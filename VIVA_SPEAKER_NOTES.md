**Slide 1 — Title & hook**
Good morning, I'm Shehan Kavishka, and my research is on classifying brain tumours from MRI scans — but the real problem isn't the technology, it's the decision. When a brain MRI arrives, a radiologist must decide whether it shows a glioma, a meningioma, a pituitary tumour, or no tumour, and that single call shapes the entire treatment. It's slow, it rests on one person's eye, and mistakes are costly. So my project asks a question that goes beyond accuracy: can we build an AI system that is not only accurate, but also honest about when it isn't sure?

**Slide 2 — The problem in brief**
There are two problems here. The technical one is that most published work relies on a single model — one CNN or one pretrained network — which can be accurate but inherits that one architecture's blind spots. The trust problem is that even accurate models are black boxes: they hand back a class but never say how confident the reasoning was, and never flag the cases where they're essentially guessing. My framework attacks both at once — higher accuracy by combining diverse models, and a new way to measure and surface uncertainty.

**Slide 3 — The dataset**
I used the Kaggle Brain Tumour MRI dataset — roughly 9,000 scans across the four classes, already split into official training and testing folders. I kept that split throughout, so every model is judged only on images it has never seen. The classes are reasonably balanced — around 1,700 to 1,850 images each in training — which matters because I don't want the model to shortcut by always predicting the most common class. Crucially, I never touch the testing folder during training; it's used exactly once, for the final numbers.

**Slide 4 — Preprocessing: one recipe for every model**
Before any model sees a scan, every image goes through the same preprocessing recipe, so I'm comparing models fairly rather than comparing preprocessing tricks. Each image is read in colour, resized to a fixed square, converted from OpenCV's BGR order to RGB because the pretrained models expect RGB, then turned into floating-point numbers and normalised so the pixel values are small and training stays stable. I deliberately use two input sizes: the standalone deep models run at 256×256 for maximum detail since they do the whole job alone, while the feature-extraction backbones run at 128×128, because there I only need a compact feature vector and the smaller size keeps thousands of images fast and memory-friendly.

**Slide 5 — Custom CNN baseline**
I started simple to establish a baseline: a custom CNN with four convolution-and-pooling blocks that progressively learn edges, then textures, then tumour-shaped patterns, followed by dense layers that make the four-class decision. Trained from scratch on the MRIs it reached about 91 to 94% on the test set — genuinely good — but it's still one architecture's opinion, and it began overfitting after around ten epochs. That result is exactly what motivated the rest of the project: a single model, however well-tuned, wasn't going to be enough on its own.

**Slide 6 — Why Xception and InceptionV3**
I added two pretrained models for two reasons: transfer learning and diversity. Transfer learning first — Xception and InceptionV3 were trained on over a million ImageNet photos, so they already know how to detect edges, curves, textures and shapes, which is exactly what a tumour is at the pixel level; rather than learn vision from zero on only 7,000 MRIs, I borrow that knowledge, freeze the pretrained layers, and only train a small new head. And why these two specifically? Because they think differently — InceptionV3 examines each region with several filter sizes in parallel to catch both fine detail and larger structure, while Xception uses depthwise-separable convolutions that separate where a pattern is from what channel it's in. Three architecturally distinct viewpoints — my CNN, Inception's multi-scale view, Xception's separable view — means their mistakes differ, and that difference is exactly what makes an ensemble stronger than any single model.

**Slide 7 — The clever bit: cutting the dense layer for features**
This is the core of my framework, so let me slow down. A network normally ends in a softmax giving four class probabilities, but one layer earlier sits my Dense-256 layer, and at that point the network has compressed the whole MRI into 256 numbers describing what it understood — without yet collapsing that into a guess. Those 256 numbers are gold, so after training normally I cut the model there: I build a new model with the same weights whose output is that dense layer instead of the softmax, giving me a 256-number fingerprint for every image. Then I feed those deep features into classical machine-learning models — Random Forest, Decision Tree and SVM — so the deep network does the seeing and the classical model does the deciding. I get deep-learning-quality features with the robustness, speed and interpretability of classical classifiers, and three extra independent voters for my ensemble.

**Slide 8 — The 6-model ensemble**
Now I have six independent opinions on every scan: three from the deep networks predicting directly, and three from the classical models reading the deep features. For the final answer they vote — in the research notebooks it's a straight majority vote, and six diverse models voting together reached 98.2% on the test set, well above my best single CNN at 94%. The reason it works is simple: for the ensemble to be wrong, a majority of six architecturally different models with different blind spots all have to make the same mistake on the same image, which is rare. In the deployed web app I improved on this with confidence-weighted voting — I sum the six probability vectors so a model that's 99% sure counts more than one that's barely 30% sure, which also gives me a real confidence percentage to show the clinician.

**Slide 9 — The novelty: EAA-IoU**
Accuracy was never the whole goal, because a model can be 98% accurate and still dangerously confident on the 2% it gets wrong — so I built a safety layer, and this is the novel part. Using GRAD-CAM, I generate a heatmap of which brain region each of the three deep models looked at, then I ask a simple but powerful question: do the models agree on where the tumour is? I measure that overlap with Intersection-over-Union for each pair of models and average them into one score I call Ensemble Attention Agreement, or EAA-IoU. High agreement means the models are consistent and confident; low agreement means they're looking at different regions, which is a red flag even if they happened to output the same class — so the app raises a clinical escalation alert. What makes this genuinely new is that every existing explainable-ensemble method compares a model's heatmap against a radiologist's hand-drawn box, which needs expensive annotations my dataset doesn't have; mine needs no ground truth at all, because it measures whether the models agree with each other — a self-supervised uncertainty signal that works on any dataset.

**Slide 10 — The live web application**
All of this lives in a working web application, because a result stuck in a notebook helps no one. The site has two screens. On Live Diagnosis, a clinician uploads an MRI and instantly sees the predicted tumour type, a confidence bar, the GRAD-CAM heatmaps from all three models plus a consensus map of where the system collectively looked, a per-model vote breakdown so nothing is hidden, and — when the models disagree — the clinical alert to escalate. The Results Gallery gathers all my research figures in one place, so the evidence behind the system is transparent. It runs as a React front-end served by a Python backend, containerised so it can deploy to the cloud, and every screen carries a disclaimer that it's a research tool, not a replacement for a radiologist.

**Slide 11 — Results & contribution**
To put numbers on it: my best single model was the CNN at 94%, and combining six diverse models by voting pushed that to 98.2% on unseen test data. But the accuracy is only half the story — the real contribution is the honesty layer, EAA-IoU, which turns the ensemble from a black box that can be confidently wrong into a system that raises its hand and says "this one is ambiguous, get a human." From my literature review, that specific combination — a confidence-weighted ensemble plus an annotation-free inter-model agreement signal — is not present in any published brain-tumour paper.

**Slide 12 — Close**
So to close where I began: one model can be confidently wrong. My framework replaces that single opinion with six diverse ones, and then, crucially, checks whether they truly agree — not just on the answer, but on the reasoning behind it. That is how you get accuracy and safety together in a clinical tool. Thank you — I'm happy to take your questions.

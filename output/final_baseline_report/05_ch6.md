# Chapter 6: Discussion, Conclusions and Recommendations

This chapter summarises the findings, evaluates the hypotheses and objectives, compares the results with the literature, states the limitations and future work, and closes with a personal reflection.

## 6.1 Summary of Findings and Hypothesis Evaluation

Five findings stand out.

1. **A small custom CNN beat the frozen pretrained networks.** The custom CNN reached 90.44% accuracy, ahead of Xception (87.62%) and InceptionV3 (86.62%), with about one sixth of their parameters. It also produced the best deep features for classical classifiers.
2. **Deep features transform classical classifiers.** Moving from 65,536 raw pixels to 256 deep features raised the SVM by 7.44 points and the Decision Tree by 8.62 points, and cut training time from up to 97 minutes to a few seconds.
3. **The six-predictor ensemble was the most accurate system.** The majority vote reached 92.50% (macro F1 0.924), 2.06 points above the best single model. Confidence weighting gave 92.25%. The number of agreeing votes was a strong reliability indicator: unanimous decisions (72% of images) were 98.44% accurate.
4. **Glioma is the weak class.** Glioma recall was 77.50%, with most misses predicted as meningioma and 23 predicted as no tumour.
5. **Attention agreement signals errors, but confidence signals them better.** Misclassified images had less than half the mean EAA-IoU of correct ones (0.0471 against 0.1029), and low EAA-IoU detected errors with an AUROC of 0.7525. Ensemble confidence reached 0.9127. The CNN's highly localised maps meant that the score was driven mainly by the two pretrained networks.

| Hypothesis | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| H1. Pretrained networks achieve higher accuracy than the custom CNN, as classifiers and as feature extractors | CNN 90.44% vs Xception 87.62% and InceptionV3 86.62%; CNN features best for every classical classifier | **Not supported** (with frozen backbones) |
| H2. The six-predictor ensemble outperforms any single member | Majority vote 92.50% vs best member 90.44%; highest macro F1 (0.924) | **Supported** |

**Answer to the main research question.** On this dataset, with frozen ImageNet backbones, transfer-learned networks did not outperform a standard CNN for feature extraction: the custom CNN gave the most discriminative features and the best standalone accuracy. Integrating all the models in an ensemble did improve accuracy and, more importantly, dependability, because the agreement among the six predictors and among the three networks' heatmaps identifies which predictions should be trusted less.

## 6.2 Comparison with the Literature

Table 27 places the results beside figures reported in related studies. These studies used other datasets (often the three-class dataset of Cheng et al., 2015), other splits and sometimes cross-validation and fine-tuning, so the figures show a range rather than a ranking.

| Study | Method | Data and validation | Reported accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minz and Mahobiya (2017) | GLCM texture features + AdaBoost | MR images, hand-crafted features | 89.90% |
| Cheng et al. (2015) | Tumour region augmentation and partition | Three-class T1 dataset | 91.28% |
| Zhou et al. (2019) | DenseNet + recurrent network | Brain MRI sequences | 92.13% |
| Deepak and Ameer (2019) | GoogLeNet features + SVM / KNN | Three-class dataset, five-fold cross-validation | 98% |
| Rehman et al. (2020) | Fine-tuned VGG16 | Three-class dataset | 98.69% |
| Talo et al. (2019) | ResNet34 transfer learning | Normal vs abnormal, 613 images | 100% |
| **This study** | **Six-predictor majority vote** | **Four classes, 1,600 held-out test images** | **92.50%** |

Table: Table 27. Reported accuracy in related studies (as reported by the authors; datasets and protocols differ)

The results are in the range of studies that used hand-crafted or partially adapted features, and below studies that fine-tuned deep networks end to end. This agrees with the finding that the frozen backbones were the main limitation of the pretrained models here: Rehman et al. (2020) and Swati et al. (2019) report their best results only after fine-tuning. The improvement of classical classifiers through deep features confirms Kang, Ullah and Gwak (2021), who found that pretrained-network features combined with classical classifiers, especially SVMs, work well for brain MRI. The success of a purpose-built CNN against larger pretrained networks echoes Badža and Barjaktarović (2020). The ensemble gain agrees with Dietterich (2000) and Ganaie et al. (2022), and the small gain from the last members illustrates their point that diversity matters more than the number of members.

For explainability, the CNN's scattered heatmaps, partly outside the brain, are consistent with benchmarks reporting that saliency maps often fail to localise abnormalities reliably (Arun et al., 2021; Saporta et al., 2022). Unlike Aasem and Javed Iqbal (2024), who compared fused activation maps with expert bounding boxes, EAA-IoU needs no annotations, and to the author's knowledge its use as a per-image escalation signal in a hybrid brain tumour ensemble has not been reported before. Its moderate performance relative to confidence supports the caution of Ghassemi, Oakden-Rayner and Beam (2021) that explanations should be treated as additional evidence rather than as guarantees.

## 6.3 Limitations

- **One public dataset of two-dimensional slices.** The images come from one public collection without scanner, hospital or patient information, so the results may not transfer to other settings (Zech et al., 2018; Kelly et al., 2019). Slices from the same patient may also appear in more than one image, and public collections assembled from several sources can contain near-duplicates and source-specific cues (Geirhos et al., 2020); a systematic audit of these effects is outside the scope of this report and is recommended below.
- **Frozen pretrained backbones and fixed training budgets.** The pretrained networks were not fine-tuned, the CNN overfitted after about six epochs without early stopping, and the transfer models were still improving at epoch 10. The comparison therefore reflects these specific training choices rather than the best each architecture could achieve.
- **Single evaluation on one test set.** Results come from one training run per model and one held-out test set of 1,600 images, without repeated runs or cross-validation, so small differences between models (for example the 0.25 points between the two voting rules) should not be over-interpreted.
- **No pixel-level ground truth.** Without tumour masks, it is impossible to measure whether the heatmaps actually cover the tumour; EAA-IoU measures agreement between models, not correctness of localisation.
- **EAA-IoU is dominated by one pair.** Because the CNN's maps are much more localised than those of the pretrained networks, the score mostly reflects the Xception-InceptionV3 pair, and the adopted alert threshold flags about half of all images.
- **No clinical evaluation.** The qualitative strand planned in the Interim Report (feedback from healthcare professionals) was not completed, so the usability and clinical value of the prototype are untested.

## 6.4 Recommendations and Future Work

1. **Clinician validation study.** Ask radiologists to review a sample of cases with and without the prototype's heatmaps and alerts, and measure their accuracy, confidence, reading time and trust. This would complete the mixed-methods design of the Interim Report.
2. **Pixel-level segmentation masks.** Use datasets with expert tumour outlines, such as multi-sequence glioma datasets, to measure how well each network's Grad-CAM overlaps the real tumour, and to check whether EAA-IoU agrees with localisation accuracy.
3. **3D and multi-sequence MRI.** Classify whole volumes with several sequences (T1, contrast-enhanced T1, T2, FLAIR) rather than single slices, so the model sees the tumour's full extent, as radiologists do.
4. **Better adaptation of the pretrained networks.** Unfreeze the upper layers of InceptionV3 and Xception with a low learning rate, add data augmentation and early stopping, and repeat each experiment with several seeds.
5. **Data audit.** Check public datasets for near-duplicate images between training and test folders and for source-specific cues before reporting accuracy.
6. **Better agreement measures.** Normalise heatmaps to a common resolution or compare their top-ranked regions, so that the CNN contributes meaningfully to EAA-IoU, and combine EAA-IoU with ensemble confidence and vote agreement in one escalation rule.
7. **Deployment.** Optimise the models for fast inference on standard hospital hardware and integrate the prototype with image-viewing systems, subject to regulatory approval as decision-support software.

## 6.5 Conclusion

This project built and evaluated an integrated framework for four-class brain tumour classification from MRI. Under one standardised pipeline, it showed that a compact custom CNN outperformed frozen ImageNet-pretrained networks, that deep features greatly improve classical classifiers, and that a six-predictor ensemble gives the best accuracy (92.50%) together with useful reliability information. It introduced EAA-IoU, an annotation-free measure of whether different networks attend to the same region, and showed that low agreement is associated with errors, although less strongly than ensemble confidence. The NeuroScan web application brings these elements together so that a clinician sees not only a prediction but also where the models looked, how they voted and when they disagree. The system is a research prototype for decision support, and its next step is evaluation by clinicians.

## 6.6 Personal Reflection

This project changed how I think about evaluating machine-learning models. My earliest notebooks each used their own image size, normalisation, labels and train-test split, and some figures were not computed on the official test folder. Results that looked impressive were therefore not comparable. The most valuable lesson of the project was the work of standardising everything into one pipeline with one configuration and one untouched test set. The resulting accuracy is lower than some of my early prototype figures, but it is a number I can defend and reproduce.

Practical problems taught me resilience. Colab sessions disconnected during long training runs, so I added per-epoch checkpoints and a stage log that let training resume instead of restarting; the custom CNN was resumed twice. Training the SVM on raw pixels took more than an hour and a half, which made the efficiency argument for deep features very concrete.

Building NeuroScan taught me that a model is only useful once someone can use it. I had to separate the work into a React front end and a FastAPI back end, make the server load the trained models from Hugging Face in the background so the site stays responsive, package everything in one Docker image and deploy it on Railway. Making the site compute Grad-CAM and EAA-IoU in exactly the same way as my analysis code, and read its results page from the same result files, was harder than I expected, but it means the demonstration and the report cannot drift apart.

The explainability work was the most interesting part. I expected the most accurate model to have the most convincing heatmaps, and I expected glioma to show the least agreement between networks; both expectations turned out to be wrong. Learning to report these results honestly, including the fact that my new EAA-IoU score is weaker than plain ensemble confidence, was an important part of becoming a more critical researcher. If I repeated the project, I would plan the clinician evaluation from the start, fix the training budget with early stopping, and choose a dataset with tumour masks so that the explanations could be checked against ground truth.
